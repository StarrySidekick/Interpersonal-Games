# The First Time I…

> You trade first-time stories. No scoring, no winner, no end condition. It is
> the best long-call game there is.

**Works over:** voice
**Time:** 15–40 min, or until someone falls asleep
**Energy:** low
**Players:** 2

## How to play

1. One of you names a first: *the first time I got in real trouble. The first
   time I flew alone. The first concert. The first time I quit something.*
2. **Both** of you tell your version. The person who named it goes second —
   they've had longer to think, so they owe the harder story.
3. The second teller names the next first.
4. Repeat until you're out of firsts or out of night.

## The rules that matter

- **Both people answer every prompt.** If only one person tells stories it's an
  interview, and interviews are exhausting to be on either side of.
- **Namer goes second.** Small rule, big effect — it stops people from picking
  prompts they've already got a polished anecdote for.
- **Ask one follow-up before moving on.** One. Not five. "What happened after?"
  is usually the right one.

## Starter prompts

The first time you… were genuinely proud of yourself · lied and got away with
it · saw the ocean · lost something that mattered · were the oldest person in
the room · cooked for someone · were somewhere you didn't speak the language ·
realized your parents were people · got paid for something · were scared of a
person · changed your mind about something big · felt like an adult · heard
your favorite song.

## Variations

- **The last time I…** — same game, recent memory. Easier, less mythic, more
  current.
- **The first time I met you** — one round, both of you, on the same event.
  The differences are the point.
- **Chronological** — go in order of age. Turns the night into two parallel
  biographies, which is a strange and good thing to have done together.

## Why it works

You already know each other's greatest hits. Firsts are indexed differently in
memory — asking for one pulls up stories neither of you has told, even after
years. And a shared prompt makes it a trade rather than a confession, so
nobody ends up feeling exposed alone.
