# The 36 Questions

> Arthur Aron's closeness protocol, run over a phone call. Not really a game —
> a structured 90 minutes that reliably makes two people feel closer. Treat it
> as a dare you both agree to.

**Works over:** voice, video
**Time:** 45–90 min, in one sitting
**Energy:** high — this needs real attention
**Players:** 2

## What it is

In 1997, Arthur Aron and colleagues published a study on generating
interpersonal closeness in a lab. Participants worked through 36 questions in
three sets, each set more personal than the last, then held four minutes of
eye contact. It works on strangers, and it works on people who've known each
other for twenty years — differently, but it works.

The questions are widely published; the Aron paper ("The Experimental
Generation of Interpersonal Closeness," *Personality and Social Psychology
Bulletin*, 1997) is the primary source, and the *New York Times* "36 Questions
That Lead to Love" (2015) is the version most people have seen. Look them up
rather than working from a half-remembered list — the ordering is the active
ingredient.

## How to run it remotely

1. **Both of you commit to the whole thing.** Ninety minutes, no multitasking.
   Bailing at question 19 is worse than not starting.
2. **Both answer every question.** One person asks, both answer, then swap who
   asks. Never let it become one-directional.
3. **Do the sets in order and don't skip.** The escalation is the mechanism.
   Set I is small talk by design; it isn't filler.
4. **Take the breaks.** Fifteen minutes between sets. Get water. Come back.
5. **The four minutes at the end**: on video, hold eye contact for four
   minutes with the camera. On voice, do four minutes of silence on the line
   together instead. Both are uncomfortable. That's the point — do it anyway.

## The rules that matter

- **No advice, no fixing, no jokes to break tension.** Especially in Set III.
  The deflection instinct is strong and it's the only thing that can ruin this.
- **You can decline one question each.** Knowing you have an out makes people
  answer more, not less. Using both your declines early is a sign to stop.
- **Don't do this on a night either of you is already raw.** It is a good
  experience, not a therapeutic one.

## Variations

- **Set I only** — 12 questions, about 25 minutes. A complete, pleasant
  evening on its own, and the right way to try this the first time.
- **One set a week** — three calls instead of one. Loses some of the
  escalation, gains a lot in actually getting finished.
- **Re-run it yearly** — the same questions, a year apart. The differences in
  the answers are the real document.

## Why it works

Aron's finding was that escalating, *reciprocal* self-disclosure produces
closeness — not disclosure alone. The structure removes the two hard parts:
deciding what to ask, and deciding whether it's your turn to be vulnerable.
Everything is pre-agreed, so neither of you has to be the one who made it
serious.
