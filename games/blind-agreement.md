# Blind Agreement

> Twelve options, the same twelve on both your screens, and you have to land
> on the same one without agreeing which.

**Works over:** voice, with a phone each
**Time:** 30 seconds a round, ten minutes a sitting
**Energy:** low — this one works when you're both nearly asleep
**Players:** 2

**[Play it](../play/blind-agreement/)**

## How to play

1. One of you gets a room code and says it out loud. The other types it in.
   Same code, same game — the two phones never talk to each other.
2. A question appears with twelve options. Both of you see the same twelve.
3. Pick one. Don't say which. Lock it in.
4. On three, say it out loud. Both at once.
5. **Matched?** Round over. Your score is the number of passes it took.
6. **Missed?** Both of your picks come off the board. Go again with what's
   left.

## The rules that matter

- **You're allowed to talk.** You're on the phone the whole time and nothing
  stops you saying anything you like before you lock in. How much you give
  away is a thing the two of you work out, and where you settle says more
  than the score does.
- **Both picks come off, not just one.** That's what gives a round its
  shape — the board shrinks fast, and the last few options are the funny
  ones.
- **Say it genuinely at the same time.** The entire game is one honest
  second. Hearing them start and adjusting is possible, obvious, and ruins
  it.
- **Fewer passes is better, and nobody wins.** There's no opponent here. The
  number is a thing the two of you did.

## Variations

- **Out loud, no board** — no phones at all. You each say a word. Then you
  each try to say the word that sits between your two. Repeat until you both
  say the same word at the same time. Same game, no screen, and possibly
  better.
- **No talking first** — lock in the moment the board appears. Faster,
  harder, and a different game.
- **Say why** — after a miss, each of you gets one sentence on why yours was
  obviously correct. Adds a minute a round and most of the laughing.

## Why it works

It's thirty seconds long, so it fits in any gap in a call, and it asks for
almost nothing on a night when neither of you has much. What it's actually
measuring is whether you can find the same idea from the inside — not
whether you like the same things, but whether you can guess where the other
person's head went. That number moving over months is the point; a single
round is just a nice noise.
