# Show Me

> A game that only works on video: your partner names a thing, you go find it
> in your home and tell its story.

**Works over:** video
**Time:** 10 min
**Energy:** medium — you have to get up
**Players:** 2

## How to play

1. Player A names a category. *Something older than you. Something you've
   fixed. Something you keep meaning to throw out. The ugliest thing you own.*
2. Player B has **60 seconds** to find something in their home that fits and
   bring it to the camera.
3. They show it and tell its story — where it came from, why it's still here.
4. Swap. Player B names the next category.

## The rules that matter

- **The 60-second clock is real.** Without it people search for the perfect
  object and the game becomes curation. The half-considered answer is always
  the better one.
- **It has to be in reach.** No "well, at my parents' house there's a…" The
  constraint is the game.
- **You have to say why it's still there.** Anyone can show an object. The
  story of why it survived every move and cleanout is the actual content.

## Starter categories

Something older than you · something that was a gift · something you'd grab in
a fire · something you bought and regret · something with someone else's
handwriting on it · the most-used thing in the room · something you don't know
how you acquired · something you'd never show a guest · a thing you own two of.

## Variations

- **The tour** — one person carries the camera through a whole room and
  narrates. Less a game, more a housewarming for someone who'll never visit.
- **Guess whose** — for households of more than one person: show an object,
  partner guesses which housemate it belongs to.
- **Photo version, async** — one category a day, one photo each. Works across
  a time zone. See [Photo Tennis](photo-tennis.md).

## Why it works

Distance makes people's daily surroundings invisible to each other — you can
talk every day for a year and never see their kitchen. This game moves the
call out of a face-sized rectangle and into a place, and objects are an easy
excuse to tell stories you'd never have found a reason to tell.
