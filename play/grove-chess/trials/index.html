Hikari Garden

Piece trials

Choose the pieces and the board. The trials try every way to stand them, the same army on both sides, play each layout against itself many times, and say which are balanced, which look fun, and whether any is playable at all.

The army

One side’s pieces, in order, left to right; the other side gets the same. Tap one to take it out. Every army has one King: it is what each side protects.

The board

Weave in a piece

A new piece, or one you invented in the pieces: the trials swap it in for each kind of piece in the army above, one at a time (and add it, if the row has room), find the best layout of each, and say which way it fits, if any.

How the trials work

Every layout is the army along your back row (or two rows, if it needs them), the King on the back row; theirs is the same, either mirrored (each piece opposite its twin, like chess) or turned (read the other way, as if the board were spun round). A layout and its mirror image are the same game, so only one is tried.

First the cheap checks: can anything be taken on the very first move, and can the side that moves first take the King by force inside three moves? Then two copies of one bot play each layout against each other. The bot looks two moves ahead (a minimax search with alpha-beta pruning, the oldest idea in computer chess), then keeps following captures until the position is quiet, so it does not stop counting halfway through a trade. A little luck in each game keeps them from all being the same.

The measures follow Cameron Browne’s Ludi (2008), a program that invented board games and judged them by self-play: balance, how often a game is decided, length, drama (the lead changing hands, comebacks), uncertainty (how late the winner pulls ahead), and here also whether skill pays, whether every piece plays, and variety. They are a bot’s guesses. Play the layouts that look good: your opinion outranks theirs.
